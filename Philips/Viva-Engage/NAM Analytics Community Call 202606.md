Tip 1: The Devil's Advocate
Default AI behavior: validate you. Agree. Make you feel smart.
That is dangerous when you are making a real decision.
The fix: explicitly assign AI the role of challenger.
"Do not tell me this is a good idea. Your job is to find the holes."
When to use:
Before you send the proposal
Before you commit to the plan

Before you present to leadership

Try It Now, Paste This in Copilot

Act as a critical thinking partner, not a supportive assistant.
Decision I am considering: "My team should replace our weekly 30-minute status meeting with a shared dashboard that updates
automatically from our project tool."
Do NOT validate this. Instead:
1. What are the 2 strongest reasons this fails?
2. Who gets hurt by this change that I am not thinking about?
3. What is the version of this that backfires in 3 months?

4. What would I need to be true for this to work?
Be direct and specific. No hedging.

Tip 2: The Pre-Mortem
A post-mortem happens after failure. A pre-mortem happens before.
You tell AI: "Assume this already failed. Explain why."

This forces the model into failure-mode reasoning, not optimism.
It surfaces risks you will not see when you are excited about an idea.
Works on: project plans, new processes, org changes, tool rollouts.

Run a pre-mortem on the following initiative.

Initiative: "Our analytics team is rolling out a self-service reporting tool so business users can build their own dashboards
without submitting requests to us."
Assume it is 6 months later and this initiative has failed.

Tell me:
1. The 3 most likely reasons it failed
2. Which failure was most predictable from the start?
3. What early warning sign would have shown up in month 1 or 2?
4. What should the team have done differently in the first 30 days?

Be specific to a healthcare analytics context. No generic advice.

Tip 3: Stakeholder Simulation
You have rehearsed a pitch in your head.
But you rehearsed against a friendly version of your audience.
AI can play the hard version: the skeptic, the budget-conscious VP, the resistant end-user.
This is not about generating content. It is about exposing gaps in your argument before the real
room.
"Respond as [specific person/role] would. Do not be nice."

Tip 4: Assumption Surfacing
Every plan has hidden premises. Things you believe are true but have not tested.
You do not notice them because they feel obvious, until one breaks.

AI is good at this because it does not share your organizational context.
It questions what you take for granted.
"What am I assuming that might not be true?"

I am going to describe a plan. Your job is to surface the hidden assumptions: things I am taking for granted that might not be

Plan: "We are consolidating 4 regional dashboards into 1 enterprise dashboard. We will standardize metrics definitions, migrate
users in waves over 8 weeks, and deprecate the old dashboards after migration."
For each assumption you find:

1. State the assumption clearly
2. Rate how risky it is if wrong (High / Medium / Low)
3. Suggest one question I should ask to validate it
Find at least 5 assumptions. Look beyond the obvious ones.

Tip 5: Scenario Modeling
Plans are built on today's conditions. Conditions change.
"What could go wrong?" is too vague to be useful.

Better: name a specific variable change and trace the consequences.
"What happens to [plan] if [variable] changes to [value]?"
This turns AI into a simulation engine for your decisions.

I have a plan. I want to stress-test it against 3 scenarios.
Plan: "My team of 6 analysts is delivering a new executive reporting package by end of Q3. We have committed to weekly
iterations with stakeholder review every 2 weeks."
Model these scenarios:
1. What happens if 2 of my 6 analysts get pulled to a priority project for 4 weeks?
2. What happens if stakeholder reviews slip to monthly because execs cancel?
3. What happens if leadership adds 3 new metrics mid-project that were not in scope?

For each: what breaks first, what is the second-order effect, and what should I pre-decide NOW?

Tip 6: The Second Opinion
AI has a confirmation bias problem: it defends its first answer.
If you ask "is this good?" after it wrote something, it will say yes.
The fix: explicitly force it to argue against itself.
Give it a position, then say "Now argue the opposite. Be equally persuasive."
This breaks the echo chamber effect and surfaces tradeoffs you would miss with a single pass.

Try It Now, Paste This in Copilot
I'm pasting this in Teams chat, copy it from there.
Take this position and argue it strongly:

"Analytics teams should embed analysts directly into business units rather than keeping them in a centralized team."
Give me 3 strong arguments FOR this model.
Now flip. Argue the opposite with equal conviction:

Give me 3 strong arguments for keeping analysts CENTRALIZED.
Finally: what is the real tradeoff I am making either way? What does each model sacrifice that the other provides?

Tip 7: Confidence Calibration
You have challenged your thinking. Now: how much should you trust what AI told you?
AI states everything with the same confidence. A verified fact and a guess sound identical.

You cannot tell the difference unless you force it to rate itself.
"Rate your confidence. HIGH = well-documented. MEDIUM = plausible but uncertain. LOW = you
generated this."
Rule: anything rated MEDIUM or LOW needs human verification before you use it.

Answer this question: "What are the top 3 reasons healthcare analytics teams fail to deliver value to the business?"
After your answer, do a self-audit:
For each claim, rate your confidence: HIGH / MEDIUM / LOW
HIGH = widely supported by published research or established practice
MEDIUM = plausible, commonly discussed, but I cannot cite a specific source

LOW = I generated this based on pattern matching, not verified knowledge
Then tell me: which of your claims would you want me to verify before putting it in a presentation to leadership?
