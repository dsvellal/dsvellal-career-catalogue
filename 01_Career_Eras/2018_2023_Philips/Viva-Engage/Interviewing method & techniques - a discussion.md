1 Context
Competence, Confidence, Courage, Creativity, Curiosity - these are some of the key
factors that a hiring manager ideally looks for in a candidate. It is important to strike
a balance of these behaviours in a potential hire for Philips so that the candidate can
productively engage in a fruitful career with Philips. To gauge these (and other
necessary behaviours), interviewers adopt different techniques. Some interviewers

prefer pair working sessions, while others prefer an evaluation interview, some
discussions are open-ended while some require the candidate to demonstrate a
specific set of skill or knowledge, some interviews are free-flowing, some of them are
time-bound and structured. There are many different structures and techniques that
interviewers at Philips have adopted that works best for them and for their team.

This page gives you a glimpse of structure and techniques adopted by some of the
bar-raiser interviewers at Philips, and intends to call out the pros and cons of the
different techniques used.

2 Why should I adopt a method for my interview?
Interviews are intense discussions, where, in a short period of time, the interviewer is
trying to assess the candidate's capabilities. Many times, as interviewers, we get so
involved in the conversation that either we end up not covering all the topics that
we'd want to cover or we extend the duration of the interview.

At Philips, we strive to balance the act of providing the best candidate experience
when they interview with Philips, and as interviewers, be able to collect all the data
that we would need, with respect to the capabilities being assessed, to come to a fair
and objective opinion about the candidate.

Having a structure to the interview helps in channelling the discussion through the
different topics that we'd want to cover, maintain focus, provide the candidate with
sufficient information that he may seek, and provide a standard interview
experience.

3 A sample method for the interview
While this section and the subsequent sections talk about (where ever the context
presents) about a technical interview, the structure can be extended to any other
type of interviews too (Manager, HR, Leadership etc.).

The below method intends to provide a guide to the flow of discussion that an
interviewer can adopt during the interview process, and focuses at every step,
making the candidate aware of what is being discussed, why we do it and helps us
collect a structured set of data that we'd want to evaluate/assess, also focuses on
providing a good candidate experience.
Note: This is just one of the many formats. Feel free to share your structure with us
via the comments below.
The sample methods' structure consists of the following sections

Context setting, Introduction, Behavioural interview, Technical assessment,
Conclusion
We'll look at each of these sections, and try to call out different techniques that
interviewers use in each of these sections.

3.1 Context setting
Many candidates are anxious about what they are going to experience in the
upcoming discussion. They would have a lot of questions but would be hesitant to
ask. Context setting is going to help them mentally prepare for the upcoming
discussion. In this section, interviewer explains to the candidate what's in store for
this discussion by talking through the different sections of the interview and explain
to the candidate what is expected in each of the sections. It is generally a good

practice to keep the discussion interactive and offer to answer any questions the
candidate has at any point in time, and make the candidate feel comfortable about
asking questions.
A sample context setting discussion can start with greeting the candidate and
exchanging pleasantries, and then just talking through each of the sections of the
interview. In our case, talking through the introduction, behavioural interview,
technical assessment and conclusion, and then encouraging the candidate to ask
any questions that he/she may have.

3.2 Introduction
In this part of the interview, information is shared with the candidate about the
interviewer, their relationship to the hiring team, the job role - its challenges and
growth opportunities, about Philips and it's focus on software development. Please
be aware that, in some of the interviews, the candidate would be presented with
information about Philips, the job-role and its expectations. In some cases, the

candidate may have had prior conversations with the HR and/or other interviewers
and would have a good idea about the above mentioned topics as well.

3.2.1 Interviewer introduction
Sometimes the profile of the interviewer may also be provided to the candidate and
it is quite possible that the candidate would have gone through social media and
other public references about the interviewer and may also be curious about some
of the unique work / references about the interviewer. It's polite to lead and
introduce ourselves (as interviewers) first and provide some contextual information
upfront, and open up the discussion for questions. Some of the topics to consider
can be:
Background of the interviewer
Current role the interviewer performs
Interviewer's competencies
Relationship of the interviewer with the hiring team
What keeps the interviewer invested in Philips
The challenges and growth opportunities that the interviewer perceives
This sets the precedence of proactive communication and opens up the discussion
for questions.

3.2.2 Introduction about Philips & the job-role
For external candidates, it is important to understand our story, our mission and how
they can contribute to that should they decide to join Philips. In this section, we talk
about introducing Philps, briefly also mention why software development is big at
Philips, talk about the job-role, identify challenges and the growth opportunities that
the job-role brings. There are many ways of having this conversation and we'll
discuss two. A leading questions technique and the guided conversation technique.
We'll discuss both the techniques below.

3.2.2.1 Leading questions technique
Many a time since the information is made available to the candidate, as an
interviewer to gauge the understanding of the candidate about Philips, the sort of
work we do, the job role and its expectations, it's good to lead with a few questions,
and have an open conversation. A few sample open-ended question would look
something like this:
Are there any questions that I can answer about Philips?
Are there any questions that I can answer about the job-role and its
expectations?
Why would you want to apply to Philips?
What do you like about the role?
Pros: In this approach, we'll know how much background work the candidate has
done. Has the candidate understood the expectations, did the candidate ask and
clarify questions with the HR, is the candidate able to articulate what he has
understood as the expectations of the job. This also shows proactiveness of the
candidate and his interest in the company & the team that the candidate wishes to
join.
Cons: Many of the candidates may not be vocal and they may need some support
during the initial conversations to start contributing towards the discussion. This

may also put the candidate in a spot if they are not ready with the information the
interviewer wishes to seek.

3.2.2.2 Guided interview technique
Through this technique, the interviewer proactively provides the information about
Philips, why software development is big at Philips, about the job-role its challenges
and growth opportunities and opens the discussion for questions or clarifications.
Having a scripted conversation helps the interviewer to take control of the
information being provided to the candidate and to the candidate, provides all the
necessary details that the interviewer intends to share.
Pros: Put's the candidate at ease, provides an opportunity to correct any
misunderstandings that the candidate may have about the topics discussed.
Cons: The interviewer will not be able to gauge the eagerness and proactiveness of
the candidate wrt understanding and articulating what the candidate understands
wrt the topics being discussed.

3.3 Behavioural interview
Behavioural interviewing focuses on a candidate’s past experiences by asking
candidates to provide specific examples of how they have demonstrated certain
behaviours, knowledge, skills and abilities. Answers to behavioural interview
questions should provide verifiable, concrete evidence as to how a candidate has
dealt with issues in the past. This information often reveals a candidate’s actual level
of experience and their potential to handle similar situations in your organization.
Behavioural interview questions tend to be pointed, probing and specific.

Philips expects each of its employees to consistently exhibit, through their work, the
five cultural behaviours: https://www.careers.philips.com/global/en/our-culture
Customer First
Quality and integrity always
Team up to win
Take ownership to deliver fast
Eager to improve and inspire
In our potential hires, it is important that we see elements of these behaviours
exhibited by the candidates in their current/past work. This will the interviewers and
the hiring panel, confidence about the candidate's cultural fitment.

To assess the behaviours, a common technique used is STAR based
interviewing. STAR stands for Situation, Task, Action and Result. In this type of
interview, a candidate is asked to narrate an experience from their current/past work,
where specific behaviour, knowledge, skill or ability is expected to be demonstrated
by the candidate on the job. STAR based questions generally start with "Tell me
about a time when ......". This provides opportunity to the candidate to pick their best
example and illustrate to the interviewer the situation, task, action and result.

3.3.1 Continuous learning
Continuous learning and development are critical to software organizations. At
Philips, we believe in continuous growth & development. Philips provides its
software employees with top-notch resources to support competency building and
stimulate learning culture. As technologies evolve, techniques evolve, so should we.
It is very important for us to also consider the "curiosity-to-learn" about our
potential hire. Here again, there are many ways of identifying this. Use the STAR
technique and ask questions, like:
Tell me about a time when you learnt something new and implemented it in
your project.
Tell me about a time when you jumped into a project that you didn't have
complete information about, and pulled through
Tell me about a time when you failed, what did you learn, how did you adopt
the learning in your new project
Tell me about some of the blogs that you follow or write
Tell me about the video channels you follow, or the podcasts that you follow,
and how do you drive the learning into your work
How do you make sure to stay up-to-date on new technologies?
What did you learn, enjoy and apply in the last month?
How would you rate the importance of learning for you?

3.4 Technical Assessment
In this part of the interview, the candidate's technical capabilities are assessed. As
always, different interviewers use different techniques to assess the technical
capability of the candidate. This can include all or a combination of the following
capabilities: Problemsolving, Coding, Design, Testing, Deployment, Dev-ops etc.

In a technical assessment discussion, it's important to keep in mind the following
aspects:
Does the candidate understand the problem, if no, does the candidate ask
questions to get clarity on the problem
Does the candidate arrive at a method to solve for the problem
Does the candidate work towards continuously improving the solution and
make the right trade-offs based on data.
Some of the techniques used to arrive at an opinion about the capabilities
mentioned above, include: Assignments, Pair working, Assisted assignments. Let's
look at each of the techniques in detail.

3.4.1 Solving assignments technique
In this technique of technical capability assessment, the candidate is provided with a
problem and is asked to come up with a solution for the problem with
minimal interaction between the candidate or the interviewer.
Based on when the problem is provided to the candidate the discussion can take
different paths. Example:
The problem can be provided to the candidate before the interview schedule
date where he gets sufficient time to solve for the problem, and then during
the assessment, presents the solution, and the interviewer discusses
strategies, trade-offs, design decisions, testing capabilities, deployment
capabilities etc.

The problem can be provided to the candidate during the interview, and he
may then be given time to solve the problem on their own and then the
candidate meets the interviewer for a discussion.
The problem can be provided to the candidate during the discussion with the
interviewer and the interviewer observes the techniques used by the
candidate to arrive at a solution for the given problem.
Each of the above chosen paths has its own pros and cons and presents
opportunities for the interviewer to observe different data points about the
candidate. Generally, this technique is used when we are evaluating for a specific
skill, which becomes evident when the problem gets solved. Eg: Candidates ability to
code in a particular programming language, the ability to identify edge cases for a
given problem etc.

3.4.2 Pair working technique
In this technique of technical capability assessment, the candidate and the
interviewer embark on a journey together to solve a problem. This is a free flowing
format, where the focus is mostly on how both the candidate and the interviewer
interact with each other, what trade-offs do they make, what design choices are
discussed etc. As almost always, there may not be a single most optimal solution to
the presented problem, but the journey towards reaching a solution provides a
wealth of information to the interviewer about the candidate. This type of technique
can also provide behavioural data points as well and can add to the observations
that'll help the interviewer in arriving at a decision. Generally, a pair working session
is time intensive and can extend to anywhere between 2hrs to 4hrs depending on
what is being discussed.

3.4.3 Assisted assignment technique
This is a pseudo pair-working session, where the interviewer presents a problem to
the candidate and assists the candidate in arriving at a solution by having
continuous conversations with the candidate. In this technique, the interviewer gets
to understand the direction a candidate approaches to solve for a problem and can
provide the right guidance to the candidate in arriving at a solution. The

interviewer can also steer the conversation in the direction he/she wishes based
on are any specific data points being observed.

Here are sample videos on how, in an assisted assignment, the interviewer tries to
navigate the discussion towards pushing the candidate into arriving at a solution
that is more optimised than the one originally provided by the candidate. The videos
start with introducing the problem to the candidate, requesting the candidate for
calling out a pseudo-code solution, optimising the solution a bit, improving the
complexity of the solution, coding for the agreed solution, pushing the boundary
and looking for opportunities of optimisations in the coded solution

3.5 Conclusion
It is important to properly conclude the discussion with the candidate and provide
an opportunity for the candidate to clarify any questions that they may have.
Providing constructive feedback and concluding on a positive note, irrespective of
the hire/no-hire decision helps in providing a good candidate experience and
enforces the candidates' opinion that the interview paid close attention throughout,
and has made enough notes/points to talk as an advocate of the candidate. It is also
important to seek feedback from the candidate about what went well and what
could be improved, and provide information to the candidate about the next steps
of the interview.
The conclusion can be split into three sections:
Summarizing the discussion
Seeking candidate feedback
Communicating the next steps to the candidate

3.5.1 Summarizing the discussion

Summarizing the discussion helps the candidate understand what the
interviewer has understood from the current conversation. In addition, it helps clarify
any misunderstandings and helps the interviewer confidently present the case of the
candidate better, in front of a panel.
Here's a sample video on how to summarize the discussion (based on the assisted
assignment technique - used before)

3.5.2 Seeking candidate feedback
Continuous feedback is essential to a good software culture. At Philips, continuous
feedback has become a critical part of how we work, as it will help us to understand
what we are doing well and where we still can develop. It is important to ask for
feedback from the candidate at the end of the interview about the way the interview
was conducted, so we could further improve the candidate experience, also learn,
and continuously improve ourselves.
Here's a sample video on how to seek feedback from the candidate

3.5.3 Communicating the next steps to the candidate
The candidate will be anxious about their performance in the interview. Also, while
our final decision is important, the candidate may have other offers to consider.
Agreeing on a period for providing feedback helps to set expectations upfront. Our
goal is to provide a candidate with a final decision in maximum 2-3 days. In case we
have other candidates to consider in the pipeline for this role, adapt the final
decision date accordingly.
