What is Heartstyles?

Heartstyles is a character development tool that measures the principles
and thinking driving behaviour in individuals, leaders and organisations.
Heartstyles is a journey of developing strength of character through selfawareness. As our character is being developed, our true potential is
released. It is based on the premise that, in order to understand and change
behaviour, we must first examine our cognitive thinking and the attitudes of
the heart that underpin and motivate what we say and do.
The Heartstyles Indicator measures eﬀective (functional) or ineﬀective
(defensive) behaviour. It measures our view of ourselves (our self-concept),
and also measures how others view our behaviour. Simply, it measures the
eﬀectiveness of how we conduct our life. It is a catalyst for growth rather
than a tool to judge. In that respect, it is not a personality profile. It is a life
indicator, not a type indicator—it measures where you are not what you
are.
Instead of looking at a personality type, preference type or skill based
measurement, Heartstyles focuses on character development and the
subsequent displayed behaviour. The Indicator provides a catalyst for
people to work with the process of self-awareness to bring about personal
character growth, thus leading to more effective performance.
The Heartstyles Indicator provides 360 feedback, enabling others to
contribute to your results by answering the same survey. This feedback can
provide insight into how accurately you see the eﬀectiveness of your
behaviour, and provide insight between how you think you behave and how
others perceive you.Your results are presented diagrammatically on a
Heartstyles Indicator as part of this Personal Development Guide.
Welcome to the journey of personal and professional development for life
eﬀectiveness. We congratulate you on having a brave heart to go on the
journey to unlock more potential that can lead to achieving your dreams
and vision in life.
People do the best they can with the knowledge and wisdom they have at
the time. Heartstyles provides a framework that reveals, enlightens and
educates people in eﬀective living. It also identifies the ineﬀective thinking
and behaviour that can hold people back. O en, “What got you here, won’t
get you there”. Living an eﬀective life requires change, development and
transformation. Our ‘gold within’—our best self—can be covered by old
habits of ineﬀective behaviour. We trust Heartstyles will unlock insight to
set you on the path for greater things.

What is Heartstyles?

Heartstyles values
The Heartstyles Indicator is a tool for development, not assessment. Your
results are not describing who you are, they only describe the behaviour you
are using. Where your results show ineﬀective behaviours, you are not a
‘bad person’. These behaviours are o en used as coping strategies to
promote and protect yourself. Moving forward, the questions you can ask
yourself are:
“What are the consequences of using this behaviour?”
“Do I want to keep living this way?”
We all have our eﬀective strategies in life. We also have our defense
strategies that we revert to under stress and tiredness.

Where is my heart?
The Heartstyles Indicator is divided into four quadrants that reflect four
universal spiritual principles driving behaviour: Humility, Love, Pride and
Fear.

HUMILITY: blue quadrant
Humility isn’t weakness for people to walk over you, it’s strength for people
to walk with you. The blue quadrant behaviours are about making it
happen, driving results and achieving goals with a spirit of humility and
openness. Humility gives space for your heart, mind and character to grow.

LOVE: red quadrant
Love is also described as passion, caring, loyalty, respect and honour. There
are diﬀerent types of love. People love sport, food and wine diﬀerently to the
way they love their partner and children. Love is also being prepared to give
‘tough love’ (feedback and coaching) when developing others.

PRIDE: green quadrant
Negative pride is o en referred to as ego and self-importance and is ‘me’
focused. Positive pride is proud of others and achievements—it’s love. If
one’s heart and mind are full of negative pride it leaves no space for growth.
The prison of pride attaches itself to proving and performing, blocking self
and others in reaching their potential.

FEAR: orange quadrant
Fear is deeply rooted in our humanity. The fear of others’ criticism, being
wrong and failure are examples of how fear holds us back from our
potential. The fortress of fear can overwhelm and people can become
trapped and blocked from living to their potential.

Understanding the Indicator

The Heartstyles Indicator measures 16 specific styles of thinking and
behaviour and positions them in a four quadrant graph.

Where is my focus and my behaviour?
The horizontal axis of the Indicator represents two separate focuses:
Personal Focus and Others Focus.
The Personal Focus side of the Indicator is made up of the HUMILITY
Personal Growth quadrant in blue and the PRIDE Self Promoting quadrant
in green.
The Others Focus side of the Indicator is made up of the LOVE Growing
Others quadrant in red and the FEAR Self Protecting quadrant in orange.
The vertical axis of the Indicator represents two separate focuses: Eﬀective
Behaviour (Above the Line) and Ineffective Behaviour (Below the Line).

Above and Below the Line
Eﬀective Above the Line behaviour represents a focus on the growth of self
and others.
Ineﬀective Below the Line behaviour is characterised by a concern for the
promotion and protection of self.

Reflection point
The easiest way to begin interpreting your Heartstyles Indicator is to ask
yourself:
“Where am I scoring Above the Line and where am I scoring Below the
Line?”
“What implications does this have for my personal and professional life
and my impact on others?”

Interpreting results

Interpreting the graph
Reading your Heartstyles Indicator is easy. Basically, ‘the bigger the bar the
bigger the behaviour’. That is, the more extension on a bar, the more that
behaviour is being seen (by yourself or by others).

The shaded zone
When a bar is in the grey shaded zone, it means it is a development
opportunity. When a bar is not in the shaded zone, it is a strength/asset.

Effective (Above the Line) styles
A bar in the shaded zone shows a style is under the 50th percentile, meaning
it is low compared to the norm group, and is thus a development
opportunity. Ideally, an eﬀective person or leader has all eﬀective (Above
the Line) styles above the 50th percentile (and not in the shaded zone).
Ineffective (Below the Line) styles
A bar in the shaded zone shows a style is over the 50th percentile, meaning it
is high compared to the norm group, and is thus a development
opportunity. Ideally, an eﬀective person or leader has all ineﬀective (Below
the Line) styles below the 50th percentile (and not in the shaded zone).
All 16 behaviour styles work together to influence individual thinking and
behaviour, thus the positive behavioural eﬀects of a higher score on one of
the Eﬀective behaviours can be easily overshadowed by a higher score on
one of the ineffective behaviours.

Interpreting results

Heartstyles scoring
In completing a Heartstyles Indicator survey, 75 items (single words or
phrases) are answered that describe thinking and behaviour. The items are
designed to assess whether a participant’s behaviour is eﬀective or
ineffective.
The 75 items are individually ranked on a five point scale (see right)
according to how accurately it describes you.

0 = Almost never
1 = Rarely
2 = Sometimes
3 = Quite often
4 = Almost always

Indicating which items are ‘Almost never’; ‘Rarely; ‘Sometimes’; ‘Quite
o en’; or ‘Almost always’ will generate results about your thinking and how
your behaviour is operating.
Your answers are then compared to a norm group to obtain a percentile
rating which is presented as your score on the graphed Indicator.

Interpreting the graph
The percentile ratings
The scoring and norming structure of the Heartstyles Indicator is such that
the 50th percentile is a key marker in the behaviours measurement, for both
the Above the Line and Below the Line styles.
Effective (Above the Line) styles
In the eight Above the Line styles, scoring less or equal to the 50th percentile
(in the shaded zone) will exponentially mean that the behaviour is not seen
a great deal—it is seen ‘Sometimes’. An eﬀective behaviour that is seen
‘Sometimes’, will not be contributing significantly to someone being seen
as consistently effective (scoring ‘Quite often’ and ‘Almost always’).
Ideally, our research shows that aiming to have all eight Above the Line
styles at least at the 50th percentile or higher (75th percentile), would
contribute to the person being seen as consistently effective.
Ineffective (Below the Line) styles
In the eight Below the Line styles, scoring equal to or more than the 50th
percentile (in the shaded zone) will exponentially mean that the behaviour
is seen ‘Sometimes’ to Quite o en’. An ineﬀective behaviour that is seen
‘Quite o en’ will be contributing significantly to someone being seen as
ineffective—it will be magnified.
Thus, for the Below the Line styles, eﬀective people and leaders would be
scoring well under the 50th percentile in order to be seen as consistently
effective.

Processing results

Situation + Thinking = Behaviour
Heartstyles uses our model and formula of S+T=B to explore the question
“Why do I do the things I do?”
For every situation, based on our thinking, we will behave in a particular
way. So, as you read your report, look at your results in the context of
S+T=B. Do I behave this way all the time, or only in certain situations? If so,
why? Ask yourself, “What is my thinking in this situation? What is happening
for me when I behave this way?”, and “What is triggering me in this
situation to behave this way?”
This may help you identify triggers that may be occurring in certain
situations (but not others), and could be causing you to behave in ways you
may not have realised.

Responses to feedback
The personal development journey isn’t always an easy one. There will be
times you gain awareness about yourself that could shock you. If you find
yourself in this position remember that you could be going through a very
normal journey of processing your results.
Stages in this processing of your results could include: shock, anger,
anxiety, rejection, resentment, rationalisation, awareness, acceptance and
action. If you find yourself identifying with a particular stage of this process
remember that the best way forward is to communicate ‘where you are at’
and ‘why you feel the way you do’ with someone. Talking about your
perceptions can usually lead to a different perspective.

Processing results

Your Self Score and Others Score results
The following section of your Personal Development Guide contains the
feedback from your (self-set) Benchmark Indicator, your Self Score results,
and your Others Score (your chosen respondent group/s) results.
Your self-perception as seen in your Heartstyles Self Score Indicator is
important for understanding how you see yourself and your intentions. The
Others Score Indicator (respondents) results, however, are important for
understanding how others see the outward behavioural results of your
intentions—how you behave.
Your nominated respondents answered the Heartstyles Indicator survey on
how they perceive you to behave, a view that may or may not be the same
as your own self-perception. It is important to remember S+T=B, and that
sometimes our intentions may not be eﬀectively communicated through our
outward behaviour as seen by others.
Our formula of S (Situation) + T (Thinking) = B (Behaviour) is important to
keep in mind. For every situation, we will behave in response to it based on
our thinking. How we think in a given situation will depend on our past
experiences—we will have positive or negative memories of similar past
experiences, and these can consciously or subconsciously influence how we
respond to our current circumstances.
So, we may find ourselves thinking and behaving eﬀectively in some
circumstances, yet become fearful or defensive (and not be our best selves)
in others. This is normal in life.
Remember, your respondents interact with you based on their perceptions
of your behaviour, not your intentions.

Reflection point
When on the development journey, remember your strengths. It’s easy to
focus too much on the development opportunities and lose heart for the
journey. The most eﬀective way to reduce Below the Line behaviour styles is
to admit they are there, get coaching and focus on increasing Above the
Line behaviour styles.

Authentic behaviour focuses on character development,
being real and transparent.
The heart attitude of Authentic

My identity is in who I am not what I do. My character as a person is my
identity and what I do is an outcome. I am courageously living my values,
humbly being transparent showing the ‘real me’ and I know my true north.
My self-control radiates calm command and significance.
The Authentic style measures the extent to which you are committed to values, integrity
and being genuine. By showing who you really are to others, you will create trust and be
respected as a leader who can be counted on to live out of integrity and principle. Your
self-awareness of the importance of personal responsibility means you are focused on
character development. Others will recognise you as someone who has intentionally
worked on developing their inner core of character. You will tend to show emotional and
physical resilience dealing effectively with pressure, and remaining optimistic and
persistent, even in adversity.
Self-aware and open, scoring High in this style suggests you are willing to be vulnerable
and share your feelings, strengths and struggles with others. You may have a strong level
of congruence—with no need to ‘bluff’, so people know they will get a straight and
constructive answer or perspective from you. You will tend to have a high level of humility
and freely acknowledge mistakes or change your opinion when you believe it is
appropriate.
Increasing Authentic behaviour

Practice keeping perspective and maintaining calm composure—this gives confidence to
those around you. Note your triggers to certain Below the Line behaviours and practice
managing those reactions so you learn to respond rather than react.

Transforming:
Personal Growth Quadrant

Transforming behaviour focuses on personal growth
through continual learning and development.
The heart attitude of Transforming

I am brave enough to be vulnerable and my heart is open and hungry to
listen and learn from others’ and experience—it seeks wisdom. I want to
improve myself for the greater good and believe I can endure through
change to do so. I radiate a safe place because I understand we are all
learning in life.
The Transforming style measures the extent to which you demonstrate a strong and
active interest and commitment to learning, personal and professional growth. You tend
to be committed to transforming your thinking and the attitudes of the heart, not just
temporary cognitive or behavioural change. This type of learning and growth is more

The heart attitude of Striving

My heart wants to achieve excellence but its identity is seated in a deep
need to be perfect, right, and have an answer for everything. I need to
perform and be ‘right’ to be OK with myself and others. If I make a mistake
my heart feels not ‘good enough’. Things will fall apart if I stop ‘doing’. I
can radiate anxiety and stress while looking like an achiever.
The Striving style measures the extent to which you are trying too hard to prove yourself
as being ‘worthy’ and competent. Striving is the counterfeit of Achieving, and we can
convince ourselves we are thinking and behaving in achieving. Usually however, there is a
‘hollow victory’ when we are striving as we never feel satisfied.
You may be setting yourself unrealistic goals, creating self-induced stress. You may tend
to want to plan everything in excruciating detail, making sure you know exactly what will
be happening at any given time. As the self-induced stress builds up, you may tend to
become angry and abrupt with others, and angry at ‘the circumstances’ for putting you
in this position. You may then find you become angry and resentful, or just plain
exhausted by ‘having to do everything in this place’. You may by now genuinely believe
that things will fall apart if you stop ‘doing’. This may not be true and is a common deceit
of this defensive and dysfunctional thinking and behaviour.
You may unknowingly be subscribing to the ‘outside-in’ approach to getting acceptance
from ‘I am good because of what I do’. You may find that you often don’t feel good
enough—so you may spend your life striving to find the perfection you may seek. When
you fail to find it, you may become resentful of yourself and others. Because you may not
value yourself, you may fail to value others and blame them for their ‘failures’.
This may unfortunately alienate others around you who may bear the brunt of your
striving behaviour. This underlying control and the stress you may unknowingly give out
may make people feel ‘on edge’, making the environment a tense rather than easy one.
Scoring strongly (50% +) in this style suggests your adoption of striving behaviour may
originate in your self-worth and identity being attached to how well things are done and
how right you are. You may experience an excessive concern with avoiding mistakes,
subsequently you may spend your life never wanting to make a mistake—and when you
do you may not be able to admit it. You may become defensive if someone criticises you,
and may resort to blaming and excuses in order to justify yourself. You may also have low
levels of compassion for others making mistakes, especially if you feel their mistakes may
reflect on you.
You may find yourself getting upset over small details. You may feel that ‘things should be
better’, or ‘this should be done differently’. The words ‘should’, ‘must’, ‘have to’ are
striving words—watch out for them as markers to Striving thinking.

Self Protecting quadrant

Behaviour that reflects the need for others’ approval and

The heart attitude of Approval Seeking

My heart wants to be liked but may carry a lack of confidence, self-worth
and validation—my heart has a deep need to be liked. I am going to like you
so you like and validate me. I can radiate flattery and a false humility
whilst being a nice person.
The Approval Seeking style measures the extent to which you need others’ approval to
feel secure and worthwhile as a person. Approval seeking is a key restraining force to
developing an authentic character and a genuine caring for others. It involves the
underlying internal assumption that “I am ok if people like me”. We all like to feel good
about ourselves. Approval seekers, however, cannot feel good about themselves unless
they have love and acceptance from other people. They become people pleasers.
Thus, you may slide into doing things for others as a way of getting their approval. You
may go out of your way to do things for others as a way of getting positive feedback
about yourself. What you may really be doing is giving to others so your own needs are
met. This is conditional giving. You may put others before yourself, but may then feel
disappointed, even resentful, if they are not reacting the way you think they should.
Scoring strongly (50% +) on Approval Seeking may originate in a fear of rejection, with
self-worth and identity being attached to being validated by others. This may be causing
you to be constantly focused on what others think, trying hard to impress. This may lead
to becoming a people pleaser, locked into avoiding conflict or confrontation, changing
your own opinions in favour of others’, or using flattery to ingratiate yourself to others.
One of the major problems with potentially being a people pleaser is that people may
begin to wonder what the truth is of what you think. They may eventually become
dismissive of you as they may feel that you may not offer a true opinion, only one you
think they want to hear.
Approval Seeking is the counterfeit of Relating. Both are about relationships—but
Approval Seeking is really about using relationships as a means to feel good about
oneself rather than relating to others without conditions. Interestingly, Approval Seeking
can often be competing for attention in a passive way.
Using all that emotional energy thinking about how you may come across to gain
approval may become a prison, leaving you feeling exhausted.

Behaviour that reflects avoiding taking responsibility,

The heart attitude of Avoiding

My heart does not want to upset or offend anyone but it carries a fear of
failure or rejection. I don’t want to upset others so I’ll just leave it alone. My
heart can be confused, not knowing what to do next. I can radiate
uncertainty while creating artificial harmony.
The Avoiding style measures the extent to which you may be indecisive and procrastinate,
putting off difficult situations or tasks for fear of failure or rejection. You may hope that
problems will go away and take care of themselves, and you may avoid conflict by ‘laying
low’ or ‘sweeping things under the carpet’, not sharing your real opinions and being true
to your personal values. Basically, living in fear.
Fear is a response to not knowing what will happen in the future. So we spend time
imagining what will happen—and it is usually negative. FEAR can be defined as: False
Expectations Appearing Real. Your fears may not be based on reality, but on what might
happen. This thinking may effectively paralyse you and stop you from doing much at all.
This may cause others to become angry or frustrated at your apparent lack of action or
commitment.
Scoring strongly (50% +) in this style suggests your adoption of avoiding behaviour may
originate in fear of rejection. Your self-worth and identity may be attached to never
upsetting anyone and you may tend to do whatever you have to, to avoid conflicts. You
may deal with other peoples’ demands by either passively agreeing with them or
somehow deflecting them.
You may find it difficult to give honest feedback for fear of upsetting others and being
rejected. This may result in others feeling they get ‘wishy-washy’ answers that don’t get to
the heart of the matter. Ironically, people may eventually value your opinions less
because they don’t know what you really think. You may thus have become a
Peacekeeper (trying to keep the peace and avoid conflict rather than addressing it) and
not a Peacemaker (being prepared to confront issues constructively to create an effective
outcome).

Some people report a ‘sick’ feeling in the stomach or a ‘tight feeling in the chest, etc.
Whilst our mind may ‘lie’ to us through denial, our body will not—it will be trying to send
messages to us but we have learned to ignore them!
